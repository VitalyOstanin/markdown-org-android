Your notes are markdown files. The tasks in them are headings carrying the
task markers Emacs Org-mode uses — TODO, DONE, a priority in brackets, a
SCHEDULED or DEADLINE date. This application reads those files and shows what
they say about today.

Nothing is imported and nothing is converted. The files stay files: the same
notes are read by the markdown-org extension for VS Code on a desktop and by
the command-line tool both of them are built on, and every one of the three
sees the same agenda because they share the same core.

What it does

* Two layouts over the same day. A list ordered by date, and a timeline that
  stands each entry against the hour it is due.
* Overdue entries grouped by how long ago they slipped — missed repeats, the
  past week, this year, older than a year — each band foldable and counted, so
  a note kept for years does not fill the screen before today does.
* A whole band answered in one move: date them today, drop the date, or mark
  them cancelled. A missed repeat is caught up to its next occurrence instead
  of being dragged to today, and the move can be put back.
* Editing from the agenda: the keyword of a task, its priority, and moving a
  planning date. Every edit is written into the note it came from and
  committed straight away.
* A task written by saying it. One sentence — "call the doctor tomorrow at
  15:00, every week" — becomes the heading, the day, the hour and the
  repeater; said to the microphone on the agenda it is written with no screen
  in between. The rules that read the sentence are the core's, so a phrase is
  understood the same way here and on a desktop.
* An entry changed by saying what to change: "move it to Friday at 16:00 and
  make it urgent" moves the day, the hour and the priority in one sentence,
  where the buttons are three taps and two dialogs of choice.
* Repeating tasks move to their next occurrence when they are marked done,
  following the repeater in the timestamp rather than dropping it.
* Several collections of notes read as one agenda — a work repository and a
  personal one, each with its own directory and its own server, each row
  marked with the colour of where it came from.

How it syncs

Over git, to a server you name. The first sync clones, later ones fetch and
hand the server what was committed here. Nothing is merged: notes that have
moved on both sides are reported rather than silently resolved, and commits
that could not be sent stay counted until a sync gets them across.

Both transports a hosted repository offers are supported. HTTPS trusts a
certificate bundle carried inside the application. SSH pins the server by its
host key, which you are asked to accept once; the device makes its own ed25519
key pair, and the private half never leaves it.

The notes can also be kept on the device alone, with git added to them later.

What it does not do

There are no accounts, no analytics, no advertisements and no telemetry. The
token or key that reaches your server lives in the application's private
preferences, is never written into the notes, and is not carried off the
device by a backup. Nothing is uploaded anywhere except to the git server you
configured.

English and Russian throughout, including the dates and the hours, which
follow the locale of the device rather than the language of the build.
